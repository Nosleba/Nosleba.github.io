💡 Tip — Singular vs Plural
Some body parts are usually plural: eyes, ears, arms, legs, feet, hands, fingers, toes. Others are singular: head, nose, mouth, back, stomach. Pay attention when you describe pain: My eyes hurt (plural), My head hurts (singular).

Key Structures

Learn how to describe symptoms, ask about health, and give advice.

1. have / have got
StructureExampleSpanish
I have + symptomI have a headache.Tengo dolor de cabeza.
I've got + symptomI've got a cold.Tengo un resfriado.
She has / She's gotShe's got a fever.Ella tiene fiebre.
Use: have and have got are both common. Have got is more informal and frequent in British English.
2. hurt / ache
StructureExampleSpanish
My [body part] hurts.My arm hurts.Me duele el brazo.
[body part] aches.My head aches. (headache)Me duele la cabeza.
I have a [body part]ache.I have a stomachache.Tengo dolor de estómago.
Use: hurt is a verb; ache can be a noun or verb. Common compounds: headache, stomachache, earache, toothache.
🩺 3. Asking about health

🔹 What's the matter?

What's the matter? – ¿Qué te pasa?

Used to ask someone what's wrong.

👉 What's the matter? – I've got a headache.

🔹 Other questions

  • What's wrong? (¿Qué pasa?)
  • Are you OK? (¿Estás bien?)
  • Where does it hurt? (¿Dónde te duele?)

💬 Example dialogue

A “What's the matter?”
B “I've got a headache and my back hurts.”
A “Where does it hurt?”
B “My stomach aches.”
📝 More useful expressions
  • I feel sick. – Me siento mal.
  • I've got a temperature. – Tengo fiebre.
  • I need to see a doctor. – Necesito ver a un médico.
  • Take a painkiller. – Toma un analgésico.
  • You should rest. – Deberías descansar.

Dialogue – At the Doctor's

Two conversations: one at the doctor's office and one between friends. Practise describing symptoms and giving advice.

Dialogue 1 – At the doctor's

Roleplay · Doctor and patient
Doctor
"Hello. What's the matter?"
Patient
"I've got a terrible headache and my eyes hurt."
I've got = I have
Doctor
"Do you have a fever?"
Patient
"Yes, I think so."
Doctor
"Let me check your temperature. ... You've got a fever. You should rest and drink a lot of water."
Patient
"Thank you, doctor."

Dialogue 2 – Between friends

Roleplay · Friends at school
Friend 1
"You look pale. Are you OK?"
Friend 2
"Not really. I've got a stomachache."
Friend 1
"Oh no! What did you eat?"
Friend 2
"I ate a lot of pizza and ice cream."
Friend 1
"Maybe you should lie down. I'll get you some water."
🔑 Key phrases for roleplay
  • →What's the matter?
  • →I've got a headache / cold / fever.
  • →My [body part] hurts.
  • →I feel sick / dizzy.
  • →You should rest / drink water.
  • →Take a painkiller.
💡 Roleplay tip
When you are the doctor, ask open questions: "Where does it hurt?" or "How long have you felt like this?"
When you are the patient, be specific: "My throat hurts" or "I have a cough".

Pronunciation – Question Intonation

In English, yes/no questions usually have rising intonation, while wh-questions have falling intonation. Let's practise with health questions.

↗
Rising intonation for yes/no questions
Are you OK? Do you have a fever? Does it hurt here?
The pitch goes up at the end. This makes it clear you're asking a question.
↘
Falling intonation for wh-questions
What's the matter? Where does it hurt? How do you feel?
Wh-questions (what, where, when, why, how) usually have a falling intonation at the end.
🎯 Practice these health questions
  • ↗ Are you feeling OK?
  • ↗ Do you have a headache?
  • ↘ What's wrong with you?
  • ↘ Where does it hurt?
  • ↗ Does your back hurt?
